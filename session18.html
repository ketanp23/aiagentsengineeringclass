2 · The pipeline you're building

A panel, then a rewrite

A Skill is standing instructions; a sub-agent is a helper Cowork spins up on its own. Here each sub-agent is a different reviewer — run independently so their critiques stay genuinely different and you can compare them.

Why sub-agents are perfect for this: each reviewer gets its own context and persona, so the CFO doesn't parrot the EM and the editor doesn't soften the data hawk. You get four honest, independent reads — then the skill compares them. Consensus is the signal; divergence is the judgement call.

Meet the panel — four lenses, on purpose different

Reviewer 1

The Engagement Manager

A McKinsey EM reviewing a first-year analyst. Logic, structure, MECE, the "so what." Does the recommendation actually follow from the evidence?

First question: "Is your headline the answer, or just a topic?"
Reviewer 2

The Client Decision-Maker

A skeptical CFO/CMO who has to act on it. Is there a clear ask? Is the impact quantified? Does it change a decision?

First question: "On how many impressions?"
Reviewer 3

The Data-Quality Skeptic

Checks every number is defensible — volume behind each rate, no double-counting, no small-sample traps, no unsupported claims.

First question: "Show me the denominator."
Reviewer 4

The Executive Editor

Can a busy exec get it in 30 seconds? Leads with the answer? Jargon, bloat, buried lede, weak title?

First question: "What's the one line that matters?"
What the panel should catch in v1 The draft looks confident, but it quietly breaks the rules from last session. A good panel finds:
  • The headline leads with a 92% VTR on Connected TV + HD + Unruly — the exact tiny-volume trap you were supposed to cut.
  • "Mobile inventory is performing well" — a claim with no number.
  • "Top 12 placements get us to 2,450,000 impressions" — likely double-counted, shown with no running total.
  • "This will guarantee 62% VTR" — an over-claim no data supports.
  • No explicit ask — the client can't tell what decision they're being asked to make.
Why not just ask "is this good?"A single, vague "review this" gets you a single, vague answer. Naming distinct, demanding personas — and comparing them — forces specific, diverse critique and shows you which problems are real (everyone saw them) versus which are one reviewer's taste. That comparison is the skill this session builds.

4 · The two prompts

Your prompts for the run

Two prompts, run back to back in the same Cowork task. The first runs the harsh panel and compares the critiques; the second iterates to v2. Fill the blanks yourself, then open the solution to compare.

PROMPT 1

Run the harsh panel & compare

Your prompt — fill the blanks Critique the attached one-pager with a panel of  how many reviewers, run how? .
Configure them as:  persona 1 — e.g. "a McKinsey EM reviewing a first-year analyst" ;  persona 2 — the skeptical client? ;  persona 3 — the data check? ;  persona 4 — the editor? .
Each returns  what, with what severity + fix?  and a  what score? .
Then  compare how — consensus vs one-off?  and give me  what final list? .
Show solution prompt
Paste into Cowork Critique the attached one-pager (the Lumen Reach insights brief v1) with a panel of four tough reviewers, each run as a separate sub-agent so their critiques stay independent. Configure them as:
1) a McKinsey engagement manager reviewing a first-year analyst — logic, structure, is the headline the actual answer;
2) a skeptical client CFO who must act on it — is there a clear ask, is impact quantified, "on how many impressions?";
3) a data-quality skeptic — is there volume behind every rate, any double-counting or small-sample traps (verify against campaign_data.xlsx if I attach it);
4) an executive editor — 30-second read, leads with the answer, jargon and bloat.

Each reviewer returns their top 3 issues, each with a severity (Blocker / Major / Minor) and a specific fix that quotes the line, plus a score out of 5 and a one-line verdict. Then compare the critiques: show which issues 2+ reviewers agree on (fix first) versus one-reviewer-only, and give me a single prioritized fix list.

Naming four distinct personas and running them as sub-agents is what produces diverse critique. The comparison step is the learning objective — consensus issues are the real problems.

PROMPT 2

Iterate to v2

Your prompt — fill the blanks Now produce a  which version?  of the one-pager that addresses  which issues first? .
For the one-reviewer-only notes,  accept / defer / reject — and do what? .
Keep it to  what length?  and re-check the numbers so you don't  reintroduce what? .
Give me the revised one-pager plus  what record of changes?  and a  what open-items list? .
Show solution prompt
Paste into Cowork Now produce a v2 of the one-pager that addresses the prioritized fixes. Fix all blockers and every consensus issue. For the persona-specific notes, accept the good ones and note anything you deliberately defer or reject, with a reason.

Keep it to one page and the same format. Re-check the numbers so we don't reintroduce anything the panel caught — every rate must carry its volume, no double-counted totals, no "guarantee," and the shortlist must show a real running total to the 2.45M / 62% goal.

Give me the revised one-pager, plus a change log (issue → what changed, grouped Fixed / Accepted / Deferred-or-Rejected), and a short "still open" list of anything that needs data or a human decision.

The change log is what makes iteration reviewable — Raj (or the client) can see every note was addressed. The "still open" list keeps you honest about what AI alone can't close.
